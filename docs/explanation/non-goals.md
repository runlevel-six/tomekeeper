# What this deliberately will not do

Every feature a project refuses is a decision it only has to make once. This page
records the refusals and the reasoning, so that the same arguments do not have to be
had again in year three — and so that anyone deciding whether to run this knows what
they are choosing.

None of these are "not yet". The deferred work lives in the milestones; this is the
work that is not planned at all.

## Not a page snapshotter

No full-page visual capture, no WARC, no PDF rendering, no video archiving.

The thing being preserved here is **the article**: its text, its illustrations, its
metadata, in a form that reads well years later on a screen that does not exist yet.
A visual snapshot preserves a different thing — the page as it looked, including the
newsletter modal and the cookie banner — and it preserves it in a format that is
larger, harder to read, and impossible to re-derive anything from.

The archive stores the original HTML precisely so that a better extraction can be
applied later. A screenshot cannot be re-extracted. It is a photograph of a document
rather than the document.

If what you want is "the web as it was," that is [Webrecorder][] and the Internet
Archive's toolchain, and they are very good at it.

[Webrecorder]: https://webrecorder.net

## No AI features

No summarization, no auto-tagging, no recommendations, no semantic search, no chat
over the archive.

Two reasons, and the second is the one that decides it.

The first is that they age badly. A summary generated by this year's model, stored
beside an article meant to outlive the service, is a derived artifact with a shelf
life — and the archive's whole design is about which artifacts are derived and how
they are regenerated. A summary is not regenerable in any meaningful sense: rerun it
and you get a different summary.

The second is that they change what the thing is. This archive is a record of what
one person chose to read. A recommendation engine makes it a system that decides what
they read next, which is the opposite; auto-tagging makes the tags a machine's
opinion rather than a reader's. The value of a personal archive is that every
judgment in it is the reader's own.

Full-text search over what you actually read is the feature this replaces, and it is
already here.

## No native mobile client

The reader is a web application, installable to a home screen, drawing its own
navigation because a standalone window has none. That is as far as it goes.

A native client is a second implementation of every view, a release cycle, two app
stores, and a codebase that must keep pace with the one that already exists. For a
single-maintainer project it is the fastest way to end up with two half-maintained
readers.

Existing mobile readers are supported instead, through the Fever API — the reason
that milestone exists.

## No federation, sharing, or social features

No public profiles, no shared collections, no comments, no following, no
"reading with friends."

An archive of what someone reads is among the more revealing datasets a person can
assemble. Every sharing feature is a new way for part of it to escape, and a new
question about who can see what. The answer here is: one person, or the people they
have given accounts to, and nothing leaves the machine it runs on.

Multi-user (M9) is family members with their own reading lists, not a network.

## No paywall circumvention

No cookie injection, no archive-service proxying, no user-agent spoofing to look like
a crawler that gets special treatment.

The outbound `User-Agent` says what this is and offers a contact URL. It respects
`robots.txt` on article fetches. A site that does not want to be archived by this
gets its wish, and the reason it is not negotiable is that the alternative makes every
operator of this software a nuisance on somebody else's server, in their name.

A page that cannot be fetched is recorded as such, and shows up in **Attention** with
the reason. That is the honest outcome.

## Not a crawler

It fetches what a subscription brought and what a reader saved. It does not follow
links out of an article, spider a site, or discover content on its own.

The distinction matters operationally as well as ethically: the fetch budget is
bounded by what somebody chose to read, which is the property that makes politeness
achievable. A crawler's budget is bounded by the size of the web.

## Not a hosted service

There is no multi-tenant version of this, no billing, no operations team. It is
software you run on a machine you control, and the support boundary is stated in the
README: it works for the maintainer's setup, and bug reports with enough detail to
reproduce are welcome.

That is not modesty. A personal archive that a company can turn off is not an
archive, and the entire design — files on disk, a standalone page per article, an
export that reads in a text editor — is arranged so that this program failing does
not take your reading with it.

## Not a general-purpose bookmarking tool

No link rot monitoring, no tagging taxonomies with hierarchies and rules, no
annotations layer, no browser extension with a dozen capture modes.

Saving a URL archives it, exactly as a feed item is archived. That is the feature.
Everything past it is a different product, and the failure mode of building it is a
reading tool that has become an information management system nobody reads in.

## Where the line moves

These are refusals about scope, not about quality. Anything that makes the archive
more durable, more readable, or more honest about its own state is in scope by
default — that is what the milestones are for.

The test a proposal has to pass is simple: **does this make what is already stored
better, or does it store something new?** Improving extraction, preserving more of a
page, making a body replaceable when a better one appears, being clearer about what
failed — all in scope. Adding a category of thing the archive holds is where the
question gets asked properly.
